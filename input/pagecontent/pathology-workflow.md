# Pathology workflow

The pathology pathway begins when a clinician identifies a suspicious lesion during examination or imaging and places an [order for histology or cytology](StructureDefinition-pathology-order-lt-lab.html). The same step is illustrated with a [pathology order example](ServiceRequest-servicerequest-pathology-order-example.html). The request records the anatomical site, the type of procedure, and the clinical question—such as confirming or excluding malignancy.

Next, the tissue is obtained. In the illustrative case, a [breast biopsy](Procedure-procedure-breast-biopsy-example.html) is performed and material is submitted as separate [left](Specimen-specimen-left-breast-example.html) and [right](Specimen-specimen-right-breast-example.html) samples. Each sample is represented in the record as a [laboratory specimen](StructureDefinition-specimen-lt-lab.html): labelled, traceable, and sent to the laboratory under conditions that preserve tissue quality.

When the material arrives, staff perform macroscopic (gross) assessment: inspection, description, and measurement of weight, dimensions, and—when relevant—how many lymph nodes are in the excision. That information is captured as [gross specimen measurements](StructureDefinition-specimen-measurement-lt-lab.html), with an [example observation](Observation-observation-specimen-measurement-example.html). A [histology laboratory step](Procedure-procedure-histology-example.html) may be recorded separately to reflect processing in the lab.

The tissue is then fixed (for example in formalin), embedded in paraffin, and cut into a **tissue block** suitable for sectioning. A block taken from a parent specimen is modelled as a [paraffin tissue block](StructureDefinition-specimen-block-lt-lab.html); see the [block example](Specimen-specimen-block-1-example.html). Thin sections are cut and placed on slides.

Slides are stained—commonly with haematoxylin and eosin (H\&E)—and additional techniques may be used as needed. For breast disease, immunohistochemistry can include [oestrogen receptor (ER)](Observation-observation-estrogen-receptor-status-example.html), [progesterone receptor (PR)](Observation-observation-progesterone-receptor-status-example.html), and [HER2 status](StructureDefinition-her2-receptor-status-observation-lt-lab.html), which guide treatment decisions.

Under the microscope, the pathologist records how the tumour behaves and is distributed: for example focality, histologic type, and extent. Those findings align with a [tumour characterisation observation](StructureDefinition-tumor-observable-lt-lab.html), as in this [example](Observation-observation-tumor-observable-example.html). **Tumour size** on the slide (largest dimension and further dimensions) is recorded separately as [tumour measurements](StructureDefinition-tumor-measurement-lt-lab.html); see the [measurement example](Observation-observation-tumor-measurement-example.html).

For **prostate cancer**, the pathological T category, nodal (N) and distant (M) status, and tumour morphology are brought together in a single [prostate cancer diagnosis and stage](StructureDefinition-prostate-condition-lt-lab.html). Other tumour sites use analogous staging approaches outside this illustration.

Finally, findings are assembled into a [diagnostic report](DiagnosticReport-diagnosticreport-pathology-report-example.html). The [authoritative pathology document](Composition-composition-pathology-example.html) brings together clinical context, specimen and procedure information, microscopic results, and the conclusion - then reviewed, authorised, and returned to the referring clinician.
